## Elaboration

I've found both sources. Here's what the paragraph is actually drawing from:

**The Minsky reference — *Computation: Finite and Infinite Machines* (1967)**

The source is Minsky's 1967 book *Computation: Finite and Infinite Machines* (Prentice-Hall). Its preface opens: "Man has within a single generation found himself sharing the world with a strange new species: the computers and computer-like machines. Neither history, nor philosophy, nor common sense will tell us how these machines will affect us."

That's almost certainly the origin of the paragraph's phrase "man-made yet alien" — it's a paraphrase of Minsky's "strange new species" framing. The same 1967 book also listed his earlier 1965 paper "Matter, Mind and Models" (Proc. IFIP Congress) in its bibliography, which is where Minsky most directly explored minds biological and artificial as analogous systems. Both pieces are relevant to the psychology angle you mentioned.

**The Randell reference — the phrase itself is surprisingly slippery**

The pithy definition "the multi-person development of multi-version programs" is widely attributed to Brian Randell as characterizing software engineering, though Randell himself wrote that he has "no recollection of having coined such a phrase — and despite searching cannot find any evidence that would enable me to take the credit."

The phrase is most commonly traced to his 1979 ICSE paper "Software Engineering in 1968," which reflected on the first NATO Software Engineering Conference. That conference itself was initiated in early 1967, when the NATO Science Committee began discussions on possible international actions in computer science, leading to the historic 1968 Garmisch conference co-organized by Randell and Peter Naur. So the paragraph's attribution to 1967 likely refers to the year the NATO software engineering planning discussions began, not a published 1967 Randell paper.

**The specific psychology angle you're looking for**

The most psychologically oriented Minsky work from that era is his 1965 paper "Matter, Mind and Models" (Proceedings of the IFIP Congress). It directly explores how a mind — biological or computational — builds an internal model of itself and the world, and how those models might collaborate or be compared across different kinds of minds. That framing is what the paragraph gestures at with "minds collaborating beyond their individual limitations" — it's Minsky treating AI as fundamentally a psychological and cognitive question, not just a computational one.

**In short:**
- The "man-made yet alien" quote → Minsky, *Computation: Finite and Infinite Machines* (1967 preface)
- The psychological vision of minds collaborating → Minsky, "Matter, Mind and Models" (1965, IFIP Congress)
- The "multi-person construction of multi-version programs" → attributed to Randell, likely from his 1979 ICSE retrospective on the 1967-68 NATO software engineering discussions
